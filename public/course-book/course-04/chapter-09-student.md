# Adoption and Change Management

## 1. What you will learn

A technically suitable change can fail to improve work if people do not understand it, cannot use it or must absorb additional tasks without support.

Adoption is more than opening an application or attending a presentation. It means using the changed process correctly and consistently within its intended boundary.

In this chapter, you will learn to:

- Identify affected roles and changes to their work.
- Prepare clear, role-specific communications.
- Design practical user preparation using supplied business cases.
- Collect feedback and respond to concerns.
- Update process instructions without losing version control.
- Address accessibility and participation barriers.
- Distinguish communication, practice readiness and sustained adoption.

Your required practice is to prepare a change plan covering affected roles, practice, communications, feedback and process updates.

## 2. Lessons
### Lesson 1 — Identify what changes for each role

Change management is the work of helping an organisation move from its current process to an agreed new way of working.

Begin with the work, not the announcement. For each role, ask:

- What task changes?
- What responsibility remains?
- What new work appears?
- What information or support does the person need?
- What might prevent correct use?

A change that releases preparation time may add review work. A simpler Sales brief may create more questions for Operations. A new source-retrieval method may add maintenance tasks for IT.

Worked example: follow the work across roles

For Meridian’s proposed internal brief comparison:

| Role | Proposed change | Responsibility that must remain clear |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sales users | Use a template or permitted assistance method and record effort | Preserve customer requests and obtain required review |
| Sales Lead | Prepare users and resolve process questions | Accept internal briefs and customer-facing quotations |
| Operations | Provide approved source facts and resolve availability exceptions | Confirm availability rather than let drafts create commitments |
| IT | Assess access and any service dependencies | Technical permission, support and maintenance arrangements |
| Finance | Examine actual effort and operating costs | Distinguish capacity from cash savings |
| Sponsor | Decide whether to advance the proposal | Use evidence rather than enthusiasm alone |

This table does not prove that each role has accepted the work. It identifies conversations and commitments needed before the pilot.

Understand resistance as information

Resistance is reluctance or opposition to a proposed change. It may reflect uncertainty, workload, inaccessible materials, conflicting incentives or a real design problem.

Consider the statement:

“I do not want another place to type the same enquiry.”

A poor response is:

“You need to be more positive about automation.”

A useful response is:

“Which fields would you enter twice? Let us distinguish the pilot measurement record from the enquiry record and remove unnecessary duplication.”

The concern may reveal a process flaw. Do not assume the person misunderstands simply because they disagree.

Likewise, concern about job impact should receive a factual answer. Meridian’s case establishes no staffing decision or payroll saving. Its current purpose is to compare preparation methods and their resource effects.

Check L1: Why should a concern about extra review work be investigated rather than treated only as a communication problem?

### Lesson 2 — Communicate the purpose, boundary and next action

A useful communication answers:

1. Why is this change being considered?
2. Who is affected?
3. What should they do?
4. What is permitted?
5. What requires review or escalation?
6. Where can they ask questions?
7. What happens next?

Different audiences need different detail.

Sales users need practical instructions and help routes. Operations needs clear exception ownership. Finance needs effort and cost evidence. The Sponsor needs decision implications.

Avoid messages such as “AI will save everyone time”. That statement is broader than the evidence.

Worked example: improve a launch message

Weak message:

Our new AI quotation process is ready. Please use it from now on.

This is misleading because no live process is ready, the pilot concerns internal preparation, and assistance readiness remains unresolved.

Better message:

We are preparing an offline comparison of internal enquiry briefs under MSP-PILOT-002. Two proposed Sales participants will practise the brief and exception routes before any pilot round. Customer messages and live record writes are outside the boundary. The assistance arm requires confirmed access, entitlement and support. Please identify unclear instructions, duplicated work and accessibility barriers before participation is confirmed.

This message gives an accurate purpose and a useful next action.

Make communication accessible

Accessibility means that people can perceive, understand and operate the materials and controls they need.

For this business process, useful design choices include:

- Text instructions alongside diagrams.
- Clear headings and field labels.
- Status words rather than colour alone.
- An equivalent way to complete required actions using a keyboard.
- Written information alongside spoken explanations.
- Readable, structured documents rather than image-only instructions.
- A way to request an alternative format without disclosing unnecessary personal information.

W3C guidance explains that colour should not be the sole means of conveying status or prompting action.1 It also explains keyboard operability, with a narrow exception for functions inherently dependent on movement paths.2 Selecting a brief status is not such a path-dependent function.

Do not claim that a template or product is accessible merely because its design looks simple. Check the actual material and required task with the intended users and relevant tools.

Check L2: Why is “green means accepted; red means held” an incomplete instruction?

### Lesson 3 — Prepare users through realistic practice and support

Reading a guide establishes exposure to information. It does not establish that a person can apply it.

Useful practice should let users:

- Complete a normal case.
- Recognise missing or invalid information.
- Respect a permission boundary.
- Apply a stop or escalation rule.
- Record the required review evidence.
- Ask for help without bypassing controls.

A short job aid can make the process easier to remember. A job aid is a compact reference used while performing work.

Worked example: a useful job aid

For an internal enquiry brief:

1. Confirm the case and permitted sources.
2. Check product, quantity and requested date.
3. Separate requests, source facts and unknowns.
4. Prepare the internal brief or exception record.
5. Obtain version-specific Sales review.
6. Record the outcome and any escalation.
7. Pause the affected method if a stop condition occurs.

This job aid supports action. “Be careful with AI” does not explain what to do.

Use the same responsibility rules in practice and work

If practice allows users to skip review, they may learn the wrong process. If a practice case has denied source access, the correct response is to hold the source use, not find another account.

Practice feedback should distinguish:

- A knowledge gap.
- An unclear instruction.
- An interface or accessibility barrier.
- Missing access or support.
- A genuine disagreement about the process.

A person unable to operate a required control has not necessarily failed to understand the task.

Support must have a usable route

For Meridian:

- Sales Lead receives brief-process and review questions.
- Operations Lead receives availability and source-meaning questions.
- IT Lead coordinates technical access assessment.
- Technical support operators and backups remain unconfirmed.

The plan must identify that gap. An email address is a contact route, not evidence that an operational support service is staffed.

Practice effort, review, support and process updates consume resources. Keep them visible in the cost model. Do not add them twice if they are already included in Chapter 5’s setup or operating allowances.

Check L3: What is wrong with concluding that a user needs more training when a required status control cannot be operated with a keyboard?

### Lesson 4 — Turn feedback into controlled process improvements

Feedback describes how the proposed process works for the people using or receiving it.

Useful feedback identifies:

- The task or case.
- What the person expected.
- What happened or remains unclear.
- The effect on work.
- A suggested change, if available.

“Not useful” is a starting comment. “The brief has no place to distinguish requested delivery from confirmed delivery” identifies a design issue.

Close the feedback loop

A feedback loop has five parts:

1. Capture the issue.
2. Assign an owner.
3. Decide the action.
4. Check the revised material or process.
5. Tell affected users what changed or why it did not change.

Do not collect feedback and leave users uncertain about its outcome.

A process update may change instructions, fields, routes, ownership or controls. Record what changes and which version applies.

If a change affects pilot outputs or measurements materially, create a new round or controlled protocol version. Do not overwrite earlier evidence or revise criteria silently.

Measure more than attendance

Distinguish:

| Measure | What it establishes |
| --- | --- |
| Notice received | Communication reached the participant |
| Guide read | Participant reports exposure to instructions |
| Practice completed correctly | Demonstrated behaviour on supplied cases |
| Pilot tasks completed as designed | Participation in the defined pilot |
| Sustained use | Continued correct use during a specified operating period |
| Feedback supplied | Participant contributed observations or concerns |

Two people receiving a notice does not establish that both are ready to participate.

A reasonable early readiness condition for Meridian is:

Both proposed participants can demonstrate the required normal, exception, permission and stop responses using an accessible method.

This is a synthetic pilot-entry condition, not a course passing rule.

Check L4: Why should an updated template have a version identifier even if the change seems small?

## 9. Solutions and explanations

Lesson checks

L1: Extra review is real work. It may affect staffing, capacity, cost and the process design. Communication can explain it but cannot remove the resource requirement.

L2: Colour alone does not convey the meaning to everyone. Use explicit labels such as “Accepted — internal use only” and “Held — clarification needed”.

L3: The barrier concerns task operation, not necessarily knowledge. Diagnose and provide a usable equivalent route before treating it as a training issue.

L4: A version identifies which instructions and controls produced a result. Even a label change can alter interpretation and comparability.

2 recipients ÷ 2 planned participants × 100 = 100%

(4 + 2) completed ÷ (4 + 4) assigned × 100 = 75%

Correctness among completed responses:

6 correct ÷ 6 completed × 100 = 100%

1 ready participant ÷ 2 planned participants × 100 = 50%

2 contributors ÷ 2 planned participants × 100 = 100%

These results are compatible. High receipt and feedback do not imply full practice readiness.
